NBA QUANT
Unified Player & Team Intelligence + Game Prediction Engine
Combined blueprint containing the two preceding NBA frameworks: the statistical player/team intelligence
architecture and the probabilistic engine for projecting player box scores, final scores, and game winners.
Document scope: 100 universal player metric slots; 20 position-specific metric slots for each of PG, SG, SF, PF and
C; 240 team metric slots across 20 categories; a seven-layer prediction framework; possession simulation;
validation; and a staged build roadmap.
Important: This is a system-design specification, not an empirical study or a set of actual NBA forecasts. Some proposed metrics
require licensed optical tracking, custom event charting, or model estimation. Any sample predictions below are explicitly fictional.

A5. Connecting the player and team layers
Player intelligence (universal and specialized) feeds an active-lineup model. That lineup model informs team
offense, defense, possession mix, shot quality, and matchup outcomes. Team profiles feed back into the expected
opportunities and efficiency of every participant. Keep the player and team layers on a common, possession-level
source of truth.
Recommended tables: games; possessions; play_by_play; players; player_game; team_game; substitutions;
lineup_stints; shots; rebounds; passes and touches; injuries; rotation_projections; metric_definitions;
model_features; model_forecasts; forecast_outcomes.
Available data and access: NBA Stats and its glossary for box-score, advanced, tracking-summary, and shooting
definitions; play-by-play for possessions, substitutions, and lineup reconstruction; custom or licensed tracking for
defender assignments, movement, shooting gravity, shot quality and defensive schemes. Historical coverage
differs by metric and season.
Principle: record raw data, derived estimates, provider provenance, season coverage, sample sizes and uncertainty.
Do not infer missing tracking data as if it were directly observed.

PART B — PREDICTIVE INTELLIGENCE ENGINE
The objective is a single probabilistic basketball model in which projected player box scores, team possessions, final
scores and win probability are generated consistently from the same underlying game events. Rather than
independently guessing each outcome, predict playing time, opportunities, action types and their results.

B5. Rebounds, assists, threes and defensive box-score stats
Rebounds: Forecast missed-shot opportunities first. Estimate offensive/defensive rebound probabilities and allocate
credited rebounds to players on court conditional on location, boxouts, conversion skill, competing rebounders and
position. E[REB] = E[OREB] + E[DREB]. The same rebound must not be awarded twice.
Rebound inputs: Expected missed shots, rebound chances, shot location, rim distance, positioning, contested
rebound rate, teammate competition, opposing center, boxouts, lineup, minutes, pace, fouls and conversion
probability.
Assists: Model passing opportunity → teammate shot attempt → shot make → official assist credit. Use potential
assists, passes per touch, drive-and-kick rate, time of possession, PnR usage, teammate shot quality and lineup
chemistry. Recorded assists must correspond to actual simulated made field goals.
Three-pointers: Predict 3PA volume separately from make probability. Inputs include minutes, 3PA share, shot
profile, catch-and-shoot versus pull-up mix, defender proximity, shot quality, primary creator availability and
expected defensive coverage. A basic conditional model is 3PM | 3PA = n ~ Binomial(n,p); a higher-fidelity model
allows varying shot difficulty and correlated outcomes.

B6. Team winner and final-score engine
Combine (i) opponent-adjusted long-run team strength, (ii) current active lineup and expected rotation strength,
and (iii) game-specific matchup and environment. Model fitted contributions rather than hard-coding manual
weights. As a conceptual example only, one could begin with a 35%/45%/20% blend and then learn the actual
optimal structure on historical data.

Simplified expected score: Expected points = expected possessions × expected points per possession. Example:
100 possessions × 1.18 = 118 for Team A; 100 × 1.12 = 112 for Team B, a six-point expected margin. These are
synthetic values and omit the full distribution of pace, efficiency, game state and late-game behavior.
B7. Possession-by-possession simulation
1. Determine five on-court players, game clock, score, team fouls and rotation state.
2. Select action type (transition, pick-and-roll, isolation, post-up, spot-up, handoff).
3. Assign participating ball handler, passer, shooter, screener and defenders.
4. Resolve shot attempt, turnover, block, shooting foul or other event.
5. Resolve makes/misses, free throws, live/dead balls and rebound outcomes.
6. Update team score, possession, player box scores, fatigue, fouls and substitutions.
7. Continue until regulation concludes; simulate overtime when required.
All outputs must reconcile: points credited to shooters sum to the team score (accounting for all scoring rules);
assists attach to eligible made field goals; rebound counts arise from missed-shot opportunities; possession totals,
minutes, on-court status, and team results remain consistent.

B8. Monte Carlo outputs
Run many independent game simulations conditional on estimated player, team, lineup and environmental
distributions. Compute the share each team wins, expected score, median margin, total, and percentile intervals.
For each player, collect PTS, REB, AST, 3PM, STL, BLK, TOV, FGA, FTA and minutes across all simulations.
Estimated win probability = games won by Team A / total simulations. Estimated chance player i scores at least 30
= number of simulations with PTS_i ≥ 30 / total simulations. The sample size reduces Monte Carlo noise; it does not
repair a misspecified simulation.

After each game, decompose forecast error into minutes, usage, attempt volume, shot quality, shooting variance,
assist conversion, rebound opportunities, possessions, and team scoring. Do not interpret one hot shooting night as
proof of a broken model. Audit both discrimination and calibration, and monitor model drift.
B12. Build sequence
Phase 1 — Historical data foundation: Reconstruct possessions, games, substitutions, lineups, shots and source
provenance.
Phase 2 — Player forecasting: Train active status, minutes, FGA/3PA/FTA, assist and rebound opportunity models.
Phase 3 — Team forecasting: Build opponent-adjusted strength, pace, score and win-probability baselines.
Phase 4 — Unified simulation: Connect possession events and player box scores with team results.
Phase 5 — Advanced tracking features: Add shot quality, spatial gravity, coverage, assignments and lineup
interactions.
Phase 6 — Validation and production: Freeze predictions; evaluate out of sample; publish calibrated distributions
and audit logs.
B13. Finished prediction report
Ten panels to expose in the terminal
Game outcome: win probability, score, margin and total
Player box scores: PTS/REB/AST/3PM/STL/BLK/TOV/minutes
Probability distributions and threshold chances
Player-versus-defender matchup intelligence
Rotation and expected five-man lineups
Availability and minutes-restriction scenarios
Cross-stat correlations and covariance
Simulation explanation and major outcome drivers
Live in-game updates to state and win probability
Frozen prior predictions, actual outcomes and model audit
Core operating rule: project who plays, how long they play, the opportunities they receive, the types of possession
events that occur, and the results of those events. The game winner and every player’s box score should emerge
from the same coherent event history.
